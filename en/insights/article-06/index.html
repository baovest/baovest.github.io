Step 3 — Technical & Sentiment Check

  • Home /
  • Insights: The AI-Assisted Investment Workflow /
  • Step 3 — Technical & Sentiment Check

Fundamental analysis answers “what is the business worth?” Technical and sentiment analysis answers a different question: “what is the market willing to pay right now, and is momentum with you or against you?” You can be right about the fundamentals and still lose money if you buy at the wrong moment — a great company in a downtrend, breaking support, with negative sentiment, can put a fundamentally sound investor through a painful drawdown before being proven right.

Technical analysis tells you about trend (up, down, or sideways), key support and resistance levels, momentum, and whether a price move is backed by real volume. Sentiment analysis tells you about market psychology (is everyone bullish already, which is a contrarian warning, or bearish, which can be an opportunity?), crowding, news flow, and positioning. Together, they answer a narrower but important question than fundamentals do: not “should I own this,” but “is now a good time to buy — or should I wait?”

This is not day trading or an attempt to call the exact top or bottom. It’s simply asking whether the setup supports the thesis, or whether patience is the better move.

The technical analysis prompt

Analyze the current technical setup for [TICKER: COMPANY NAME].

Provide:
1. Current trend - uptrend, downtrend, or sideways?
2. Key support and resistance levels
3. Recent price action - any breakouts, breakdowns, or consolidation?
4. Volume analysis - is the move supported by volume?
5. Technical indicators - RSI, moving averages, momentum
6. Overall assessment - bullish, bearish, or neutral setup?
7. Risk/reward - where to enter, where to place stop-loss?

Use recent data (last 3-6 months) and be specific with price levels.

A few things separate a useful response from a vague one: specific price levels (“support at $485”) instead of hand-waving (“support exists below”), appropriately hedged language (“likely,” not “will”), and volume included as part of the read — a breakout on weak volume is a different signal than one on heavy volume. Treat overly confident predictions and analysis with no mention of a stop-loss level as red flags.

Worked example: NVIDIA’s technical setup

With fundamental analysis complete — a strong business, but demanding valuation — the technical read checks whether now is a good entry point. A prompt asking for trend, key levels, volume, indicators, and a stop-loss recommendation surfaces a nuanced picture: the uptrend is intact and healthy (higher highs, trading above both the 50-day and 200-day moving averages), but the stock is testing resistance after a strong six-week run, volume is weakening on the approach to that resistance level, and RSI is nearing overbought territory. The setup is bullish, but extended — the risk/reward at the current price is worse than it would be at a pullback to the nearest support level.

The takeaway isn’t “don’t buy NVIDIA” — it’s “the trend supports the thesis, but chasing the stock at resistance offers a poor risk/reward compared to waiting for either a pullback to support or a confirmed breakout on strong volume.”

The sentiment analysis prompt

Analyze the current market sentiment for [TICKER: COMPANY NAME].

Provide:
1. News sentiment - what's the recent news flow? (positive/negative/neutral)
2. Social media sentiment - what are investors discussing? (bullish/bearish)
3. Analyst sentiment - recent upgrades/downgrades and price targets
4. Institutional positioning - are funds buying or selling?
5. Sentiment extreme check - is everyone too bullish or too bearish?
6. Contrarian signal - does sentiment suggest crowding or opportunity?

Focus on the last 2-4 weeks for recency.

This is where Grok earns a place in the toolkit: its integration with X gives it real-time access to social sentiment and current price action in a way that a general chat assistant, limited to its training-data cutoff, can’t match. A prompt like “what’s the current sentiment on X about [TICKER]? Are discussions bullish, bearish, or mixed, and how has that changed over the last week?” gives you a live read on crowd psychology. Perplexity is a solid alternative for news-flow and institutional-positioning questions if you don’t have Grok access.

Combining technical and sentiment: the synthesis that matters

Running fundamental, technical, and sentiment analysis on NVIDIA together tells a more complete story than any one alone:

Analysis typeVerdict
FundamentalStrong business, reasonable valuation if growth continues
CompetitiveDominant position, moat defensible
TechnicalUptrend intact but extended, testing resistance
SentimentExtremely bullish across analysts, social media, and institutions — a crowded trade

Sentiment sitting at an extreme is itself a signal, independent of the technical picture:

  • Extremely bullish (most sources agree) — most potential buyers are already in, expectations are high, and the risk of disappointment rises. Contrarian read: be cautious.
  • Extremely bearish — most potential sellers are already out, expectations are low, room for positive surprise. Contrarian read: possible opportunity, if fundamentals support it.
  • Mixed — healthy debate, room to move either direction, often the best environment for new positions.

The combination matters more than either signal alone: a strong uptrend backed by bullish sentiment is a case for continuation, but an uptrend that’s extended and sentiment that’s near-universally bullish is a caution flag — everyone who was going to buy may have already bought, which raises the question of who’s left to push the price higher.

The verdict this step produces

Technical and sentiment analysis don’t tell you whether to own a stock — fundamentals do that. They tell you when, and at what price. A fundamentally excellent company can still be a “not yet” if the setup is extended and the crowd is already fully positioned. That’s not a rejection of the thesis — it’s patience, and it’s exactly the kind of discipline that separates a structured process from chasing whatever is trending this week.

With the business case, the timing check, and the sentiment read all in hand, the next step is the one professional firms treat as mandatory and individual investors most often skip: deliberately arguing against your own idea before you commit capital.
