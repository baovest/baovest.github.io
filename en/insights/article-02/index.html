How Investment Firms Actually Work

  • How Investment Firms Actually Work

The firm and characters below are fictitious — a teaching case designed to show how professional investors actually work. The process is real.

Most people assume professional investors have some secret sauce, a magical ability to predict the future. They don’t. What they have is structure — a defined process they run every single time, regardless of how excited or nervous they feel about an idea. Here’s what that process looks like over the course of one day at a fictional firm, Aurexum Capital, as a senior analyst named Marcus evaluates a new opportunity for portfolio manager Emma.

9:00 AM — Screening: separating signal from noise

With thousands of publicly traded companies, Marcus doesn’t pick stocks randomly. Every week he runs a screening process — a set of filters (revenue growth, margins, valuation, industry tailwinds) that narrows the universe down to 10–15 companies worth a closer look. Most candidates fail the filters immediately; that’s the point.

The ones that pass get a 30-minute “sniff test”: does the business model make sense, are the financials clean, are there obvious red flags? Only after clearing that initial check does Marcus commit real time to deep analysis. No emotional attachment — just process. Screening isn’t about finding the perfect investment on the first try. It’s about finding companies worth analyzing at all.

11:00 AM — Deep research: building a mosaic

Deep research happens in layers, not as a single pass:

  1. Financial statements — three to five years of income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow trends. Is revenue growing consistently? Are margins expanding or contracting? Is the company generating real cash, not just accounting profit?
  2. Competitive positioning — market share, customer retention, switching costs. Amateurs look at a company in isolation; professionals always look at competitive context.
  3. Valuation — multiple approaches (P/E, DCF, historical averages, peer multiples) to determine whether the stock is cheap, expensive, or fair.
  4. Technical and sentiment — what is the market actually telling you right now? Volume, key support/resistance levels, analyst sentiment, news flow.

Each layer adds a piece to the picture. None of them alone is the answer — it’s the combination that builds the case, which Marcus then writes up as a research memo: bull case, bear case, risks, and catalysts.

2:00 PM — The investment committee: attacking your own idea

At 2 PM, Marcus presents his thesis to the investment committee. Emma’s first response isn’t “great, let’s do it” — it’s “now tell me why we shouldn’t invest.” The team doesn’t try to sell each other on the idea; they try to break it. They stress-test assumptions: what happens if a major customer is lost? What if the sector is late-cycle? Marcus has to defend the thesis point by point.

This is deliberate. Bad ideas die in this room. Good ideas get better. That process — arguing against your own thesis before committing capital — is what protects the firm from costly mistakes, and it’s the single habit individual investors skip most often.

3:30 PM — From analysis to decision

Analysis tells you what might happen. A decision framework tells you what to do about it. Before entering any position, Emma answers three questions:

  • How much? Position sizing reflects conviction. A 1% position signals low conviction; 5–7% is a meaningful bet; anything larger demands exceptional conviction and limited downside.
  • At what price? Liking a company isn’t the same as buying it at any price — entry needs a defined trigger, whether that’s a technical level, a catalyst, or a valuation threshold.
  • What could go wrong? Stop-losses and exit criteria are defined before entry, not improvised after the fact.

That last point is the difference between investing and gambling: exit criteria before entry. Structure over prediction, process over outcomes — professional firms don’t predict the future, they manage probabilities and take calculated risks with defined downside and asymmetric upside.

Where AI fits in

What Marcus did in one day — screening, deep research, competitive analysis, valuation, a research memo, and a defended thesis — is six-plus hours of skilled work. Most individual investors either skip it entirely or spend weeks trying to replicate it alone.

AI changes that math. The same five-step framework Aurexum Capital runs internally maps directly onto AI tools:

  • Screening — AI scans and filters companies against criteria you define.
  • Fundamental analysis — AI reads financial statements, explains business models, and benchmarks competitors, citing its sources.
  • Technical & sentiment — AI interprets charts and summarizes market sentiment from news and social media.
  • Bull/bear debate — you can prompt AI to argue both sides. You become your own investment committee.
  • Decision & risk management — AI helps structure your decision framework and stress-test your assumptions.

The playing field isn’t leveling completely — you still won’t have insider access or institutional resources. But the analytical process, the structured thinking, the discipline that separates Marcus and Emma’s decision from a coin flip? That’s now accessible to anyone willing to learn how to use these tools well.
