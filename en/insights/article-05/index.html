Step 2: Fundamental Analysis

  • C.H. Kua
  • AI-Assisted Investment Workflow
  • October 1, 2026

Fundamental analysis evaluates a company’s intrinsic value by examining its business model, financial health, competitive position, and growth prospects. The core question it answers: is this a good business at a reasonable price?

It rests on three pillars:

  1. Business quality: what does the company do, who are its customers, what’s its moat, who are its competitors, what drives growth?
  2. Financial health: revenue trajectory, profitability, cash flow generation, balance sheet strength, return on invested capital
  3. Valuation: price relative to earnings, sales, and growth; comparison to peers and history

A few misconceptions worth discarding up front: you don’t need to be an accounting expert (basic financial literacy is enough), you don’t need to predict exact future earnings (focus on trends and probabilities), expensive isn’t automatically bad and cheap isn’t automatically good, and you don’t need proprietary data, since public filings contain most of what you need. AI can read a 10-K in seconds and benchmark it against competitors, work that used to take analysts days.

By the end of this step, you should be able to state a clear investment thesis: “I believe [Company] is a [good/poor] investment because [3–4 key reasons].”

NotebookLM for document-grounded research

Before diving into prompts, one tool deserves special mention: NotebookLM, Google’s free AI research assistant built specifically for working with your own documents. Unlike a general chat assistant, NotebookLM only uses the sources you upload. Every answer comes with a citation back to the exact document, which means it can’t hallucinate information that isn’t in your sources.

For fundamental analysis, that makes it ideal for company filings: upload a 10-K, the latest earnings transcript, and an investor presentation, then ask direct questions like “explain the company’s business segments and how the revenue mix has changed over the past two years” or “what does management say about competition, and what are the disclosed risk factors?” You get specific, cited answers instead of a general impression, turning hours of reading a 200-page filing into minutes of targeted questions. Use NotebookLM for deep, document-grounded facts; use ChatGPT or Claude for synthesis and role-playing analysis; use Perplexity when you need something more current than your uploaded documents.

Role-playing prompts

Asking AI to adopt a specific analytical role produces more structured, professional output than a generic question. It mimics how investment firms actually staff an analysis, with different perspectives checking each other.

Act as a [TYPE] analyst with 10 years of experience covering the [SECTOR] sector.

I want you to analyze [COMPANY NAME] and provide:
1. [Specific analysis request]
2. [Specific analysis request]
3. [Specific analysis request]

Be specific with numbers and examples. Identify both opportunities and risks.

Useful variations to run in sequence, each surfacing a different angle:

  • Fundamental equity analyst: business model, competitive advantages and threats, financial health, three bull points and three bear points, fair value versus current price.
  • Growth investor: total addressable market and share, growth sustainability, margin expansion potential, key drivers for the next 2–3 years.
  • Value investor (Buffett-style): durability of the moat, management and capital allocation quality, free cash flow and ROE, margin of safety at current price.
  • Risk-focused analyst: business, financial, operational, and external risks, ranked by likelihood and impact.

For comprehensive coverage in one pass, ask for all three lenses at once and a synthesis: “Give me the growth analyst view, the value analyst view, and the risk analyst view, each with an overall assessment, 3 supporting points, and the biggest concern. Then synthesize a balanced view.”

Worked example: NVIDIA’s fundamentals

Financial statements. A prompt asking for three years of trend analysis on revenue, margins, cash flow, and balance sheet health surfaces a clear pattern: explosive revenue growth driven almost entirely by the data center segment, gross margins expanding from the low-60s into the low-70s as scale kicks in, near-100% conversion of earnings to free cash flow, and a fortress balance sheet with minimal debt. The green flags (expanding margins alongside explosive growth, exceptional cash generation) come with one yellow flag worth tracking: a single segment now represents the large majority of revenue. That’s a concentration risk rather than a red flag, but worth monitoring.

Competitive positioning. A “strategy consultant” prompt breaks down the moat: a 15-year software ecosystem (CUDA) with millions of trained developers, a full hardware-plus-software stack that’s hard for point-solution competitors to match, and high switching costs from rewriting code for an alternative platform. The most credible competitive threat isn’t the obvious rival (AMD, which has trailed for a decade). It’s hyperscalers building their own custom chips for internal workloads, which shrinks the addressable market rather than taking share directly.

Valuation. A valuation-specialist prompt compares current multiples to the company’s own five-year history and to peers, then runs bull/base/bear scenarios. The verdict is nuanced: expensive relative to history and most peers, but not obviously in bubble territory once growth is accounted for (a PEG ratio near 1 suggests the multiple is roughly in line with growth). The tension is real: strong business, strong position, but priced for continued excellence, which means limited room for disappointment.

Reading AI output critically

AI-generated fundamental analysis is only as good as your verification of it. Run every response through a critical-reading checklist:

  • Verify the data. Check dates. Is this current or two years stale? Confirm key numbers against actual filings rather than trusting a stated percentage at face value.
  • Check internal consistency. Does the response contradict itself (a “strong balance sheet” followed by a “high debt levels” claim)? Does the narrative hold together?
  • Push past vague claims. “Strong market position” means nothing without a number. Ask directly: “what specific evidence supports that?”
  • Watch for one-sided framing. AI tends toward positive framing by default. If a response reads as universally bullish, explicitly prompt for the bear case.

Common AI errors to watch for: outdated training data (verify with Perplexity or fresh documents), plausible-sounding but invented metrics (verify anything unusually specific against real filings), oversimplified accounting (check whether a headline number includes a one-time item), and correlation dressed up as causation (“revenue grew because of AI”; dig into which specific products drove it).

The most reliable check is cross-referencing: run the same prompt through two different AI tools, and if they agree on the key facts, treat that as higher-confidence. If they disagree, go to the primary source, the actual filing, to resolve it.

By the end of this step you should be able to write a two-to-three sentence synthesis: what you’re confident about, where the tools disagreed, and your resulting thesis. That thesis is the input to the next step: checking whether the timing is actually right to act on it.
