Step 5: Decision & Risk Management

  • C.H. Kua
  • AI-Assisted Investment Workflow
  • October 1, 2026

Most investors can analyze reasonably well and still struggle at exactly this step: “I’ve done all this work. Now what?” A simple buy/sell/hold rating is incomplete. It doesn’t say how much to buy, when to sell, or how risk gets managed along the way. A professional decision always answers five questions:

  1. Do I invest? (Yes / No / Wait)
  2. How much? (Position size, as a percentage of the portfolio)
  3. When? (Now, scale in, or wait for a specific entry point)
  4. Exit plan? (Stop-loss, profit target, or time limit)
  5. Monitoring? (What you’ll track, and how often)

The gap between “NVIDIA is a buy” and “NVIDIA is a buy at a 5% position size, scaled in between $450–500, exit if margins fall below 65% or the stock drops 20%, with profits taken starting at $650” is the entire difference between an amateur decision and a professional one.

Position sizing: conviction times risk/reward

Position size should reflect two inputs together, not either alone. High conviction without a favorable risk/reward can mean overconfidence; a favorable risk/reward without real conviction can mean you’re missing something. Both together justify a larger position:

High conviction + favorable risk/reward   → 10-15% position
High conviction + mixed risk/reward       → 7-10% position
Medium conviction + favorable risk/reward → 5-7% position
Medium conviction + mixed risk/reward     → 3-5% position
Low conviction (any risk/reward)          → 0-2% position, or pass

The prompt template to generate a recommendation:

Based on the following analysis of [COMPANY]:

**Bull Case**: [Summary - upside potential, probability]
**Bear Case**: [Summary - downside risk, probability]
**Current Conviction Level**: [High/Medium/Low]
**My Risk Tolerance**: [Conservative/Moderate/Aggressive]
**Portfolio Context**: [Total portfolio size, existing positions, sector exposure]

Recommend:
1. Appropriate position size (% of portfolio)
2. Rationale for this sizing
3. Is this a core holding or satellite position?
4. Should I enter all at once or scale in?

For a bull/bear synthesis on NVIDIA showing medium conviction and a slightly unfavorable risk/reward at the current price, this produces a specific, defensible number. Not “moderate,” but something like 3–5% of the portfolio, entered in two tranches (an initial position now, the remainder added only if the stock pulls back toward a defined support level).

Setting the exit before you enter

The single habit that separates disciplined investing from hoping is defining the exit before buying, not after:

Design exit triggers for my [COMPANY] investment:

**Investment Thesis**: [One-sentence summary of why you own it]
**Key Bull Dependencies**: [What has to stay true for thesis to work]
**Key Bear Risks**: [What would invalidate the thesis]

Provide:
1. **Fundamental Exit Triggers**: What business metrics or events would signal thesis is broken?
2. **Technical Stop-Loss**: What price level represents unacceptable loss? (typically 15-25% down)
3. **Time-Based Review**: When should I reassess if nothing changes? (e.g., 6 months)
4. **Take-Profit Strategy**: At what price should I take partial profits?
5. **Trailing Stop**: Should I use a trailing stop as the stock rises?

A complete exit plan has two independent halves. The downside side is a technical stop-loss (typically 15–25% below entry) plus named fundamental triggers, for example “exit if revenue growth falls below 20% year-over-year” or “exit if gross margin drops below 65%.” They should be specific enough that you can check them mechanically at each earnings release, not vague enough to argue your way around later. The upside side is a laddered take-profit plan: selling a third of the position at one target, another third at a higher one, and letting the remainder run if the thesis is still intact. That locks in gains without forcing you to guess the exact top.

Common position-sizing guidelines

As a general (not universal) framework:

Core holdings (high conviction, low risk):       8-15% per position
Growth holdings (high conviction, higher risk):   5-10% per position
Speculative holdings (medium/low conviction):     2-5% per position
Experimental/learning holdings:                   1-2% per position

Maximum per sector: 25-30%
Maximum per single stock: 15-20%

And a few risk-management defaults worth adopting as habits: a stop-loss 15–25% below entry, taking 25–50% of profits off the table at a 2x gain and letting the rest run, trimming any position that’s grown past roughly 20% of the portfolio back toward 15%, and never risking more than 2–3% of total portfolio value on a single trade going wrong.

The document that ties it together: an investment memo

After going through this process, write it down in a one-page memo. Future you will forget today’s reasoning, and documentation is what lets you actually learn from outcomes later instead of rewriting history in hindsight.

INVESTMENT MEMO: [Company]

Date:                       Current Price:
DECISION:                   POSITION SIZE:

INVESTMENT THESIS (one sentence):

BULL CASE:  [upside, probability]
BEAR CASE:  [downside, probability]
BASE CASE:  [most likely outcome, probability]

ENTRY STRATEGY:
EXIT TRIGGERS (technical + fundamental):
KEY METRICS TO MONITOR:
NEXT REVIEW DATE:

What AI can and can’t do here

AI is genuinely useful for structuring the decision framework, calculating position sizes from parameters you supply, suggesting exit triggers grounded in your own stated thesis, and running scenario analysis. It cannot tell you your personal risk tolerance, predict the future with certainty, or make the final call. That responsibility, deliberately, stays with you.

A reusable checklist for every future decision

Run every investment through the same short gate before committing capital:

  • I can state my thesis in one clear sentence
  • I’ve generated and taken seriously both a bull case and a bear case
  • I’ve assigned rough probabilities to bull / base / bear outcomes
  • My position size reflects both conviction and risk/reward, not conviction alone
  • I have a stop-loss, a profit-taking plan, and named fundamental exit triggers, defined before entry
  • I know what I’m monitoring, and on what schedule
  • I’ve written a one-page memo I could hand to someone else and have them understand the decision

If you’d be uncomfortable explaining this decision to another person, or if you find yourself unable to answer “what would make me sell,” that’s a signal to slow down before you act, not after.

Good process doesn’t guarantee a good outcome on any single trade. Even a well-run process is happy with a win rate in the 55–60% range over time. What it guarantees is that your decisions are repeatable, reviewable, and improvable, which is the entire point of running an analysis this way instead of on gut feel.
