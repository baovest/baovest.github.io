Step 4: The Bull/Bear Debate

  • C.H. Kua
  • AI-Assisted Investment Workflow
  • October 1, 2026
Table of Contents
  1. Building the strongest bull case
  2. Building the strongest bear case
  3. Synthesizing both sides into a decision input

Once you’ve formed an opinion about a stock, your brain starts working against you. Confirmation bias means you notice the news that supports your view and skim past the news that doesn’t. Anchoring means once you’ve decided a price is fair, you rationalize why it’s still fair even as conditions change. Overconfidence means you assume your analysis was thorough, which is rarely true.

Professional firms combat this structurally, not through willpower: analysts present the bull case and the bear case before a decision is made, someone is explicitly assigned to challenge the thesis, and an investment committee debates before capital moves. AI is a genuinely good devil’s advocate for this, precisely because it has no emotional attachment to being right. The same model that built your bull case can turn around and build an equally strong bear case, tirelessly, as many times as you ask.

The goal of this step isn’t to talk yourself out of a good idea. It’s to reveal blind spots you didn’t know you had, stress-test the thesis before the market does it for you, and, if the bull case survives serious challenge, walk away with genuinely higher conviction instead of borrowed confidence.

Building the strongest bull case

The key instruction here is counterintuitive: don’t ask for a balanced case, ask for the strongest possible one. Balance comes later, in the synthesis step. Right now, self-censoring just produces a weaker analysis.

Act as a bullish analyst building the strongest possible investment case for [COMPANY].

Provide:
1. The core bull thesis in one sentence
2. 5-7 key reasons to be bullish (prioritized by importance)
3. What could go BETTER than current expectations?
4. Potential upside catalysts over the next 12-24 months
5. Base case and bull case price targets with assumptions
6. Why bears are wrong (counter the main bear arguments)

Be specific with data and examples. This should be the STRONGEST possible bull case.

A strong bull case has specific, quantifiable drivers rather than vague assertions, rests on multiple independent reasons rather than one load-bearing assumption, names plausible catalysts with a rough timeline, and still acknowledges risk while explaining why it’s manageable. Weak bull cases lean on a single dependency (“if AI grows, this stock wins”), assert qualities like “strong management” without evidence, or promise unrealistic upside that would require flawless execution and luck simultaneously.

For NVIDIA, a well-built bull case rests on several independent legs: AI infrastructure spending as a genuine multi-year trend rather than a fad, a CUDA software moat that’s taken 15 years to build and would be expensive for any customer to abandon, massive operating leverage as margins expand with scale, supply that’s currently more constrained than demand (a pricing-power signal), and a large untapped inference market beyond today’s training-focused revenue. None of these depends entirely on the others, which is exactly what makes a bull case robust rather than fragile.

Building the strongest bear case

Then, deliberately, argue the other side, and take it just as seriously.

Act as a bearish analyst building the strongest possible case AGAINST investing in [COMPANY].

Provide:
1. The core bear thesis in one sentence
2. 5-7 key reasons to be bearish (prioritized by risk level)
3. What could go WORSE than current expectations?
4. Downside catalysts or triggers over the next 12-24 months
5. Bear case price target with assumptions
6. Why bulls are wrong (counter the main bull arguments)

Be brutally honest. This should be the STRONGEST possible bear case - assume
you're short the stock and need to convince others.

A useful variant is the pre-mortem: “Imagine it’s two years from now and [COMPANY] has fallen 50%. What went wrong? What signs should we have seen coming? What did bulls miss?” Working backward from failure surfaces risks that a forward-looking prompt sometimes misses.

For NVIDIA, a serious bear case points to customer concentration (a handful of hyperscalers represent the majority of revenue, and they’re simultaneously building competing custom chips), the historical pattern of infrastructure cycles looking permanent right up until they aren’t, margins sitting at a cyclical high that historically mean-reverts, and a valuation that assumes several more years of uninterrupted execution with essentially no room for a miss.

The right emotional reaction to a good bear case is discomfort, not immediate rebuttal. If your first instinct is to argue with every point, you’re too anchored to the thesis already. The better question is “what if this is right, how would I know, and what would I do?”

Synthesizing both sides into a decision input

Comparing the two cases side by side, rather than picking a winner outright, is the actual point of this exercise. For each side, ask three questions: which arguments are strongest, what has to be true for that case to win, and how likely is that?

A rough, explicit probability weighting turns two narratives into one number. For example: a bull scenario with 30% upside at 25–30% probability, a base case with -10% to -30% at roughly 50% probability, and a bear scenario with -40% to -58% at 15–20% probability, nets out to a probability-weighted expected return that can be mildly negative even when the bull case sounds compelling in isolation. That’s exactly the kind of thing narrative alone would hide from you.

That synthesis usually lands in one of four patterns:

  1. Bull case wins clearly: upside meaningfully exceeds downside, bull dependencies more plausible than bear ones → larger position.
  2. Bear case wins clearly: downside meaningfully exceeds upside → avoid or exit.
  3. Mixed/uncertain (the most common outcome): both cases have real merit → small position, or wait for a better entry.
  4. Great company, wrong price: the long-term bull case is strong, but current valuation leaves no margin of safety → watchlist, wait for a pullback, or scale in slowly.

Define your trip wires explicitly at this stage: the specific, observable events that would tell you the bear case is starting to play out (a named metric crossing a named threshold), not a vague feeling that “something’s off.” Writing them down now, while you’re not yet emotionally invested in the position, is what makes them usable later.

The most important question this step answers is simple: after hearing both sides, do I still want to own this? If yes, you move to the final step, turning that conviction into an actual position size and risk plan. If no, you’ve just avoided a mistake for free, and that’s a genuine win, not a wasted afternoon.
